A multisig protects against a stolen key, not against every signer being shown the same lie

Threshold signing assumes signers are approving different views of the same truth. When the interface is compromised, M-of-N approves the same forgery M times.

Smart-contract multisigs are the default custody answer for treasuries, and they are a good one. They are also routinely described as protecting against a class of attack they do not touch.

What the contract actually checks

Signatures are gathered off-chain and verified on-chain in one call. The contract confirms that enough valid signatures exist over a specific transaction hash. That is the whole guarantee, and it is a strong one: an attacker with one key cannot move anything.

Note what it does not check — whether the signers understood what they were signing.

Two failure modes, same outcome

Concentration. Ronin had enough valid signatures because too few parties controlled too many keys. The threshold was met exactly as designed; the independence it assumed was not there.

Deception. The 2025 Bybit incident is sharper. No key was broken and every signature was valid. The signing interface showed signers a benign transaction while their devices signed a different hash. M-of-N worked perfectly and approved a forgery M times.

Threshold signing assumes each signer independently verifies. If they all verify through the same compromised surface, N signatures carry the information of one.

The part people miss in the contract

Safe supports Modules: contracts authorised to execute through the Safe while bypassing the threshold check entirely. That is the feature — spending limits, recovery flows, session keys — and it is also a second authority path that a reviewer looking only at owners and threshold will not see.

Auditing a multisig means auditing its modules. "It's 3-of-5" describes one of the ways funds can move, not all of them.

What actually closes the gap

Verify the transaction hash independently of the interface proposing it — a second device, a different client, anything not sharing a compromise with the first.

And for automated flows, put policy in a Guard, which runs before and after every transaction on the signing path and can revert even when signatures are valid. That is where limits and allowlists belong: in the contract, not in the frontend that might be lying to everyone at once.
