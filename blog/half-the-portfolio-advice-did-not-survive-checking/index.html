Half the portfolio advice I checked did not survive verification

I ran twenty-five claims about engineer portfolios through adversarial verification. Eleven died — and almost all of them were the statistics people quote most confidently.

Before rebuilding this site I did something slightly unusual: instead of reading portfolio advice and acting on it, I collected the claims and tried to kill them.

Twenty-six sources were fetched and about a hundred and twenty-five falsifiable claims extracted. Twenty-five of those got the full treatment — three independent passes each, every pass instructed to refute rather than confirm, with a claim surviving only if at most one pass could knock it down.

Eleven of the twenty-five were refuted. That ratio is the interesting part, but what died is more interesting still.

The casualties were all numbers

Every statistic about recruiter behaviour failed:

• "Recruiters spend a median of 31 seconds per resume."
• "Reviewers spend a median of 1 minute 40 seconds."
• "Engineers score resumes containing projects measurably higher."

Three votes to zero against, each one. These are the numbers that appear in nearly every "how to build a developer portfolio" article, usually without a link, often attributed to a study that says something adjacent but not that.

Round numbers about attention spans travel well because they are quotable and nobody checks them. That is exactly why they should be checked.

What did survive

Two findings held up, and both are uncomfortable.

Recruiters screening engineers predict interview outcomes at roughly chance level. That is from a study of 76 technical recruiters producing around 2,200 evaluations measured against real interview results.

Pedigree moves the needle, demonstrated work moves it less. FAANG or FAANG-adjacent experience made a candidate about 35% more likely to be advanced.

Put together, those two say something deflating about the screening stage: it is noisy, and the signal it responds to most strongly is one you cannot build over a weekend.

What I did with that

I stopped treating the portfolio as a conversion-rate problem. If screening is close to random and weights pedigree, then polishing the site is not where the leverage is.

So the site got shorter, not longer. Four case studies instead of a project grid, each stating what the decision cost. Nothing that claims a number I cannot defend.

The honest summary: a portfolio is unlikely to get you past a screen you would otherwise fail. It is very likely to matter in the conversation after someone is already interested — and that is a different artifact than the one most advice tells you to build.

The part worth stealing

The method transfers. When a claim carries a number and the number is doing the persuading, try to refute it before you act on it. Roughly half of what I checked did not survive, and none of the casualties looked suspicious beforehand.
