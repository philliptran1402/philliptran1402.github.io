Three perpetual DEX architectures, compared

One constraint, three architectures — and different things to trust.

Covers
GMX · Hyperliquid · Nado
Question
Who is the counterparty, and who do you trust?

01 — Why compare these three

All three solve the same constraint — a real order book needs millisecond latency, and block times do not provide it — and each answers differently. Put side by side, the comparison exposes what a perp DEX actually has to decide.

02 — GMX — the pool is the counterparty

There is no matching. Traders open positions against a shared liquidity pool (GLP in v1, GM pools in v2), and liquidity providers take the other side of every trade's profit and loss. Price comes from an oracle rather than from an order book, which is why v1 advertised zero price impact.

Orders are requests executed by keepers a short time later, so the fill price is the price at execution, not at submission. What you trust: the oracle, and the pool parameters.

03 — Hyperliquid — build the chain to fit the book

Rather than accept block-time constraints, Hyperliquid runs its own L1 where matching happens inside consensus. A fully on-chain central-limit order book, with validators doing the matching a centralized exchange would do on an internal server.

Integration looks more like a CEX than a contract call — REST and WebSocket, orders signed with EIP-712. What you trust: the validator set of a young chain.

04 — Nado — split matching from risk

Matching runs off-chain in a sequencer at single-digit milliseconds; the clearinghouse and risk engine stay on-chain, on Ink (an OP Stack L2). The sequencer can order and match but holds no custody and cannot forge signatures — withdrawals and liquidations all require on-chain verification.

What you trust: the sequencer for ordering and liveness, plus the L2 beneath it. The MEV argument is economic rather than cryptographic — millisecond latency and batching make extraction unprofitable, which is a claim worth checking rather than accepting.

05 — What the comparison teaches

The question is never "which is best". It is what are you willing to trust, and what does that buy: a pool's parameters and oracle, a new validator set, or a sequencer's ordering. Every perp design pays for speed somewhere, and the three pay in different currencies.
